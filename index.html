Module 01

The language of movement

In plain words

Every exercise, without exception, is just some joints bending, straightening or twisting. This module gives you the words for those movements — so instead of saying "a curl", you can say "the elbow bends".

That sounds like fussy vocabulary, but it's the thing that lets you work out an exercise you've never seen before. Learn these words once and the other seven modules become easy.

Three planes, a list of joint actions, and the habit of describing movement precisely instead of by exercise name. That's the whole module.

Anatomical position is the reference stance: standing upright, feet forward, arms at the sides, palms facing forward. Every term below assumes a body in this position.

The three planes

Imagine three panes of glass passing through you at right angles. A movement happens in a plane when the body part travels parallel to that pane.

The joint actions

These are the verbs. Learn them and you can describe any rep.

FlexionJoint angle decreases — curling the elbow, bending the knee. Sagittal.
ExtensionJoint angle increases — standing up out of a squat. Sagittal.
AbductionLimb moves away from the midline — lateral raise. Frontal.
AdductionLimb returns toward the midline — cable crossover. Frontal.
Internal rotationLimb turns inward at shoulder or hip. Transverse.
External rotationLimb turns outward. Transverse.
Horizontal adductionArm sweeps across the chest at shoulder height — pec deck.
Horizontal abductionArm sweeps out and back — reverse fly.
DorsiflexionAnkle only: toes pull toward the shin.
PlantarflexionAnkle only: toes point down — calf raise.
PronationForearm turns palm-down.
SupinationForearm turns palm-up.
Elevation / depressionShoulder blades ride up toward the ears, or pull down.
Protraction / retractionShoulder blades slide apart, or squeeze together.

Directional terms you'll need constantly: anterior front · posterior back · superior above · inferior below · medial toward midline · lateral away from it · proximal closer to the trunk · distal further from it.

The rule that fixes most confusion: classify the joint action, not the exercise. Almost no lift is purely one plane. A deadlift is sagittal-dominant; a bench press is not. Analyse joint by joint and the ambiguity disappears.

Module 02

Joints — what each one is allowed to do

In plain words

Every joint has a shape, and that shape decides what it's allowed to do. Your elbow is a door hinge — it swings one way and that's it. Your shoulder is a ball sitting in a cup, so it goes almost anywhere.

Once you know which joint is which kind, you stop being surprised by exercises. You can tell what a movement can train, and what it physically cannot.

A joint's shape decides which planes it can move in. Muscles only pull; bone geometry decides where the bone is permitted to go. That's why you cannot curl in the frontal plane no matter how you hold the dumbbell.

The technical measure is degrees of freedom (DOF) — how many planes a joint moves through independently. One, two, or three.

The six joint types

TypeDOFPlanesWhere
Hinge1Sagittal onlyElbow, knee, ankle, fingers
Pivot1Transverse onlyForearm, top of neck
Condyloid2Sagittal + frontalWrist, knuckles
Saddle2Sagittal + frontalBase of thumb
Ball-and-socket3All threeShoulder, hip
Glidingsmall, multiSlide, not swingSpinal facets, wrist & foot bones

Why the shoulder is the one that complains

The hip and shoulder are both ball-and-socket, built to opposite philosophies.

The payoff of the shoulder's design is the largest range of motion of any joint in the body. The cost is that soft tissue does the work of stability: the labrum (a fibrocartilage rim deepening the socket), the capsule and ligaments (which are slack through mid-range and only tighten near end range — so passive restraint is nearly absent exactly where training happens), and the rotator cuff, whose main job is not producing movement but compressing the head into the socket on every rep.

Separately, the scapula isn't attached to the ribcage by a socket at all — it floats on muscle. Serratus anterior, lower and middle trapezius and rhomboids aim the socket while the arm moves. Raising the arm overhead is commonly taught as roughly two parts shoulder-joint motion to one part scapular upward rotation approximate. "Shoulder blades down and back" is a cue about this structure, not the shoulder joint.

Knee valgus is not a knee action. The knee has almost no frontal-plane range. Knees caving in is hip adduction and internal rotation, often with ankle collapse, showing up at the knee. Address the hip and ankle.
Contested, read carefully. contested The old "impingement" model — tendon pinched under the acromion — has been substantially challenged. Terminology has shifted toward "subacromial pain syndrome", and placebo-controlled surgical trials reportedly found decompression no better than sham. I'd verify those trials directly rather than take my word for it. Rotator cuff and labral changes also appear on imaging in many pain-free people, and the posture–pain link is weaker than commonly claimed. Practical reading: the shoulder is load-tolerant and adaptable, not fragile. Manage load and progression rather than fearing specific movements.

Module 03

How muscle actually produces movement

In plain words

A muscle is a rubber band stretched between two bones. It can do exactly one thing: pull its two ends closer together. It cannot push.

So everything a muscle "does" follows from where its two ends are attached. Find both ends, ask what happens when the gap closes, and you've worked out its job — no memorising required.

A muscle crosses a joint and attaches to bone at both ends. The origin is the attachment on the more stable bone; the insertion is on the bone that moves. When the muscle shortens, it pulls insertion toward origin. That single sentence predicts what every muscle does.

Three ways a muscle contracts

Concentric

Muscle shortens under load. Standing up out of the squat; curling the weight up. Usually the "lifting" half.

Eccentric

Muscle lengthens while still producing force — controlling the descent. You can control more load eccentrically than you can lift. Strongly associated with delayed-onset muscle soreness. settled

Isometric

Length doesn't change; force is produced anyway. Plank, farmer's carry, your spinal erectors during every deadlift.

Roles in a given movement

RoleWhat it doesExample in a squat
Agonist (prime mover)Produces the intended actionQuadriceps, gluteus maximus
AntagonistOpposes it; relaxes or controlsHamstrings at the knee
SynergistAssists the agonistAdductor magnus assisting hip extension
StabiliserHolds a segment still so the movers can workErector spinae, abdominals, mid-trap

The same muscle changes role depending on the exercise. Hamstrings are the agonist in a leg curl, a stabiliser and hip extensor in a deadlift, and an antagonist at the knee during a leg extension. Memorising "hamstrings = leg curl" is how beginners get stuck; thinking in roles is how trainers don't.

Length–tension relationship. A muscle produces the most force near a mid-range length, and less when very short or very stretched. settled This is part of why a lift has a sticking point — though the moment-arm effect in Module 5 is usually the larger factor.

Module 05

Levers and torque — why exercises feel hard where they do

In plain words

Push a door right next to its hinges and it barely budges. Push the same door at the handle and it swings open easily. Same push — the difference is how far you are from the hinge.

Your joints are the hinges. A dumbbell weighs the same all the way through a curl, but its distance from the elbow changes as you move — so the work your muscle has to do changes too. That one idea is this whole module, and it explains sticking points, why cables feel different from dumbbells, and why some exercises "don't hit" where you expected.

Muscles don't lift weights directly. They apply force to a bone, which rotates around a joint. What matters is torque: force multiplied by the perpendicular distance from the joint to the line of the load. That distance is the moment arm.

A dumbbell weighs the same at every point in a curl. The demand on the biceps does not, because the moment arm changes as the forearm rotates. Drag the slider.

The three lever classes

ClassArrangementBody example
FirstFulcrum between effort and loadNeck extensors holding the head up
SecondLoad between fulcrum and effortCalf raise — toes are the fulcrum
ThirdEffort between fulcrum and loadMost of the body, including the biceps curl

Third-class levers trade force for speed and range: your biceps must produce far more force than the dumbbell weighs, but a small shortening moves the hand a long way. That's the design the human body overwhelmingly uses. settled

Practical consequence. When an exercise "doesn't hit" a muscle, the usual culprit is the resistance curve, not the muscle. Changing body angle, switching to a cable, or adding a band changes where in the range the load is heaviest.

Module 06

The movement patterns

In plain words

Instead of thinking "chest day, back day", trainers think in movements: push something away, pull something toward you, squat down, bend at the hips, carry something heavy.

It's a better filing system, because it makes gaps obvious. Run your week against the list below and you'll usually find one or two whole categories you never do.

Trainers organise programmes by pattern rather than by muscle, because patterns map onto how the body is built and make gaps obvious. The common list is seven.

PatternDominant joint actionExamples
SquatKnee + hip extension, sagittalBack squat, goblet squat, leg press
HingeHip extension, knee relatively stillDeadlift, RDL, hip thrust, kettlebell swing
Vertical pushShoulder flexion/abduction + elbow extensionOverhead press, landmine press
Vertical pullShoulder adduction/extension + elbow flexionPull-up, lat pulldown
Horizontal pushShoulder horizontal adduction, transverseBench press, push-up, dip
Horizontal pullShoulder extension / horizontal abductionBarbell row, cable row, face pull
Loaded carry & anti-rotationMostly isometric; frontal + transverse controlFarmer's walk, suitcase carry, Pallof press

A single-leg or lunge category is often added as an eighth, and some systems fold rotation out as its own pattern. The exact list is a teaching convention rather than a biological fact convention — its value is that running your week against it exposes what you've skipped.

The common gap. Typical gym routines lean almost entirely sagittal. Frontal and transverse work — lateral lunges, carries, chops, anti-rotation holds — gets skipped. This is a widely repeated observation in trainer education rather than a measured statistic rule of thumb, but it's easy to check against your own logbook.

Module 07

Exercise analysis — the method

In plain words

This is the payoff. Four questions you ask about any exercise — including one you've never seen, or one someone just made up — and you can work out what it trains and where it'll feel hardest.

Try answering them yourself before you tap an exercise below. Getting it half-right and then checking teaches far more than reading the finished table.

This is the skill the whole course is building toward. Given any exercise, you should be able to produce this table yourself. Four questions, per joint:

  1. Which joints move, and which are holding still?
  2. What action is happening at each — in which plane?
  3. Which muscles produce that action (agonist), and which hold position (stabiliser)?
  4. Where in the range is the moment arm longest — i.e. where is it hardest?

Work through unfamiliar exercises this way and you stop needing anyone to tell you what a movement trains. You also stop being fooled by exercise names: a "chest fly" and a "pec deck" are the same joint action with a different resistance curve, and a "leg press" and a "squat" differ mainly in what the spine and hip are asked to stabilise.

Module 08

Putting it to work — and what's actually uncertain

In plain words

Two halves. First, the handful of dials you actually turn when writing a programme — how much, how heavy, how often, how hard.

Second, and more useful: which confident-sounding claims you'll hear in gyms and certification courses are genuinely still being argued about. Knowing the difference is what separates someone who understands this from someone repeating slides.

Programme variables

VariableWhat it meansTypical guidance
VolumeHard sets per muscle per weekOften cited as roughly 10–20 sets/week for growth approximate
IntensityLoad relative to your 1RMStrength work skews heavier and lower-rep; hypertrophy tolerates a wide range
Proximity to failureReps left in reserveTraining near but not always to failure is the common recommendation
FrequencySessions per muscle per weekTotal volume appears to matter more than how it's split approximate
ProgressionAdding load, reps or sets over timeThe non-negotiable one
I'd verify these numbers. The set and rep figures above are widely repeated in the training literature but the ranges shift as new work comes out, and my information ends in mid-2026. Treat them as starting points, not settled values, and check a current primary source if you need precision.

Things commonly taught as fact that are genuinely debated

Muscle imbalance causes injury contested

Mechanically plausible and universally taught. The evidence linking specific imbalances to injury is inconsistent. Balanced pulling volume is still sensible — just not because it's proven preventive.

Posture determines pain contested

The link between resting posture and pain is weaker than certification material usually implies.

Static stretching before lifting contested

Short-term force reductions have been reported; the practical magnitude is argued over.

"Activation" studies = growth contested

EMG measures electrical activity in one session. It is not a direct measure of long-term hypertrophy, though it's routinely marketed as one.

Corrective exercise fixes movement faults contested

Movement-screen findings appear in plenty of pain-free people; predictive and preventive claims are weaker than the courses suggest.

Fascia / "anatomy trains" lines contested

An influential model with a real anatomical basis, but its training implications go well beyond what's established.

Knowing which claims are solid and which are convention is itself part of trainer-level knowledge — arguably the part that separates a good trainer from one who repeats their certification slides.

Training science 01

Why bodies change at all

In plain words

Your body doesn't improve because you exercised. It improves because you gave it a reason to, and then recovered. Training is a demand; adaptation is the reply.

Four or five principles govern the whole thing. Everything else in training — every programme, every argument online — is detail stacked on top of these.

The principles

Progressive overload

The demand must keep growing, or adaptation stops. Not necessarily more weight — more reps, more sets, better control, less rest, more range all count. settled

Specificity (SAID)

You adapt specifically to what you impose. Training heavy singles makes you good at heavy singles. Sets of 15 make you good at sets of 15. Overlap exists but it isn't total. settled

Individual variation

The same programme produces noticeably different results in different people. Published averages describe a group, never you. settled

Reversibility

Adaptations fade when the demand stops — though strength and muscle are retained longer than most people fear, and return faster than they were built.

Fatigue management

Every session produces both fitness and fatigue. Fatigue masks fitness, which is why you sometimes feel weakest right before you peak.

Adherence beats optimality

Not a textbook principle, but the one that decides most outcomes. A good programme you follow beats a perfect one you abandon.

Stimulus, fatigue, adaptation

On "minimum effective volume" and "maximum recoverable volume". contested These are popular framings from online coaching rather than established, measurable quantities. The underlying idea — there's a floor below which nothing happens and a ceiling above which you can't recover — is reasonable. The specific set numbers attached to them are not measurements of anything.

Training science 02

Programme design

In plain words

A programme is just a set of decisions, made once, written down — so you're not deciding what to do while standing in the gym.

This module is the list of decisions and the sensible defaults for each. Anatomy told you what an exercise does; this is what to do with that over three months.

Exercise order has one real rule. Whatever matters most to your goal goes first, while you're fresh. Everything else — "compounds before isolation", "large before small" — is that rule wearing a costume.

Training science 03

Progression and periodisation

In plain words

Adding weight every session works beautifully — until it stops, usually a few months in. Periodisation is just having a plan for what happens then.

It sounds like an advanced topic. It isn't. It's the difference between a programme and a list of exercises.

Ways to progress

Load is the obvious one and the least sustainable on its own. The full list: more weight, more reps, more sets, shorter rest for the same work, better range or control, or the same session performed with less effort than last time.

Double progression is the simplest workable method: pick a rep range, say 8–12. Add reps each week until you hit 12 on every set, then add weight and drop back to 8. Repeat. A beginner can run this for a long time without needing anything more complicated.

Periodisation models

ModelHow it worksSuits
LinearVolume falls and intensity rises over weeks or monthsBeginners; a single peak
UndulatingRep ranges vary within the week or between sessionsIntermediates; general goals
BlockDistinct phases each emphasising one quality in turnAdvanced; a competition date
AutoregulatedLoad adjusts session to session based on how you actually feelAnyone with inconsistent sleep, stress or schedule
Worth knowing. contested The evidence that any one periodisation model clearly beats another, once total volume and intensity are matched, is weaker than how confidently it's taught. What does appear to matter is having some structure and progressing it. Don't agonise over picking the right model.

Autoregulation: the RPE and RIR scale

Rather than prescribing a fixed percentage, you prescribe an effort level. RIR means reps in reserve — how many more you could have done. RPE is the same idea inverted on a 1–10 scale.

Deloads

A planned easy week — typically reduced volume, sometimes reduced intensity — to let accumulated fatigue clear. Common guidance is every 4–8 weeks, or when performance stalls across several sessions, sleep worsens, or motivation drops noticeably. The exact scheduling is more convention than established science convention; the principle that fatigue accumulates and needs clearing is not.

Training science 04

Exercise physiology

In plain words

Where the energy for a set actually comes from, and what quietly changes inside you over months of training.

You don't need the biochemistry. You need to know which system powers a 5-second effort versus a 5-minute one, because that's what makes rest periods and rep ranges make sense instead of feeling arbitrary.

The three energy systems

All three run all the time. The question is only which one dominates — and that's decided almost entirely by how long and how hard the effort is.

Muscle fibre types

TypeCharacterRecruited when
Type I (slow)Fatigue-resistant, lower force, highly oxidativeLow-force work, and first in almost any effort
Type IIaIntermediate — fast but reasonably fatigue-resistantModerate to high force
Type IIx (fast)Highest force and speed, fatigues quicklyMaximal or near-maximal effort

Recruitment follows the size principle: smaller, slower motor units fire first, larger faster ones join as force demand rises. settled This is why a set taken close to failure recruits high-threshold fibres even with a moderate weight — fatigue forces the recruitment that heavy load would have forced immediately.

Fibre type proportions are substantially genetic and shift only modestly with training, mostly between the two fast subtypes. You cannot train your way into someone else's physiology.

What training changes

Resistance training

Early gains are largely neural — better recruitment and coordination — before much tissue is added. Then muscle cross-sectional area increases, along with tendon stiffness and bone density.

Endurance training

Larger stroke volume, more capillaries, more and bigger mitochondria, improved lactate clearance. VO₂max rises early then plateaus; lactate threshold keeps improving far longer.

Two things commonly taught as fact. contested First, mechanical tension is widely accepted as the primary driver of muscle growth, but the additional roles of metabolic stress and muscle damage are actively argued over. Second, the idea that post-workout testosterone and growth-hormone spikes drive hypertrophy has been substantially challenged — you'll still see it taught, and training programmes sold on it.

Training science 05

Recovery, sleep and fatigue

In plain words

Training is the stimulus. Recovery is where the actual change happens. Most people manage the first carefully and the second not at all.

If you only improve one thing outside the gym, make it sleep. Nothing else in this module comes close.

The hierarchy

Sleep first, by a wide margin — duration and consistency both. Total food intake second. Life stress third, and it's a real training variable: a brutal work week genuinely reduces what you can recover from. Everything else — ice baths, massage guns, compression, foam rolling — sits far below these, whatever the marketing suggests.

Fatigue is not one thing

Peripheral

In the muscle itself. Clears relatively quickly — hours to a couple of days.

Central / neural

In the nervous system's drive to the muscle. Accumulates from heavy, near-maximal work and clears more slowly.

Connective tissue

Tendon and ligament adapt more slowly than muscle approximate timelines — which is why strength can outrun joint readiness in a fast-progressing beginner.

Psychological

Motivation and willingness to push. Real, measurable in performance, and routinely dismissed.

Overreaching and overtraining

Functional overreaching is a short-term performance dip followed by a rebound — a normal, often deliberate part of hard training. Non-functional overreaching is a dip that doesn't rebound on schedule. Overtraining syndrome is a much rarer, longer-lasting state involving performance, mood, sleep and health. The three are often conflated, and genuine overtraining syndrome is far less common in recreational lifters than the word suggests.

Soreness is a bad metric. Delayed-onset muscle soreness tracks novelty and eccentric load much more than it tracks whether a session was productive. A good session often isn't sore; a sore session often wasn't productive. settled
One worth verifying yourself. contested There are reported findings that regular cold-water immersion straight after resistance training may blunt some strength and hypertrophy adaptations. I'm not confident enough in the details to state it flatly — treat it as a reason to be cautious about habitual post-lifting ice baths, and check current primary sources if it matters to you.

Training science 06

Nutrition fundamentals

In plain words

A small number of things matter a great deal, and an enormous number of things matter very little. Most nutrition content inverts that ratio because the small stuff is easier to sell.

This module covers the fundamentals a trainer is expected to know — and, just as importantly, where a trainer's authority ends.

What matters, in order

LevelWhat it isRoughly how much it matters
1. Total energyHow much you eat relative to what you useDecides whether weight goes up or down. Dominant.
2. ProteinTotal daily intake, spread across mealsLarge, for anyone training
3. Carbs & fat splitHow the rest of the energy dividesModerate; largely preference and training demand
4. Food quality & micronutrientsWhole foods, fibre, vitamins, mineralsLarge for health, smaller for training outcomes specifically
5. TimingWhen you eat relative to trainingSmall — much smaller than commonly marketed
6. SupplementsEverything in a tubSmallest. See below.

Protein

Figures around 1.6–2.2 g per kg of bodyweight per day are commonly cited for people doing resistance training approximate, with diminishing returns above that. Spreading it across three or four meals is generally recommended over one large serving. These are population-level starting figures from the training literature — I'd verify current recommendations from a primary source, and they're not individualised to anyone's circumstances.

Supplements worth knowing about

The honest list is short. Creatine monohydrate and caffeine have the most consistent evidence behind them for training performance. Vitamin D and omega-3 may matter where someone is genuinely deficient — which is a medical question, not a training one. Almost everything else sold in the category has weak, mixed, or industry-funded evidence.

Scope of practice — read this one carefully. In most jurisdictions a personal trainer may give general nutrition guidance but may not prescribe diets, treat medical conditions, or manage nutrition for a diagnosed illness. That's a registered dietitian's role. I don't have confident knowledge of how this is regulated in India specifically, so if you ever intend to advise others, verify the position there directly rather than assuming it matches US or UK rules.

Nothing in this module is dietary advice for any individual, and it deliberately contains no calculator. Personal targets belong with a qualified professional who knows your circumstances.

Training science 07

Assessment and goal setting

In plain words

Before you program anything you need two things: a reason to believe training is safe to start, and a number to measure against later.

That's genuinely most of it. The elaborate screening protocols you'll see sold are a much smaller part of the picture than they're presented as.

Readiness screening

A health history and a readiness questionnaire — the PAR-Q is the best-known widely used — exist to catch the small number of people who should speak to a doctor before starting, not to design their training. The questions cover cardiac history, chest pain, dizziness, joint problems and current medication. Anything flagged means referral before progression, not refusal to train.

Baselines worth recording

Strength

A tested or estimated max on two or three main lifts. Estimation formulas from a rep-max are convenient but approximate, and get less accurate above roughly 10 reps approximate.

Body measures

Weight trend over weeks rather than single readings; circumference measures. Body-fat estimates from consumer devices carry large error margins — treat them as trend lines at best.

Capacity

A simple repeatable cardio test, or just resting heart rate and how a known effort feels.

Movement

Ankle dorsiflexion, overhead reach, single-leg control. Useful for choosing starting exercises.

On movement screens. contested Screening systems are widely taught as injury-prediction tools. The evidence for that predictive claim, and for the idea that correcting screen findings prevents injury, is considerably weaker than certification material implies. Screens are reasonable for choosing where to start someone. They're much shakier as prophecy.

Goal setting that survives contact with reality

Outcome goals ("add 20 kg to my squat") are motivating but not controllable. Process goals ("train three times a week for eight weeks") are controllable and are what actually produce the outcome. Good practice is to set the outcome goal, then convert it immediately into the process goals that would cause it, and track only those.

Re-test baselines every 8–12 weeks. More often than that and normal fluctuation drowns the signal.

Training science 08

Coaching, cueing and behaviour change

In plain words

Knowing the right thing to do and getting a person — including yourself — to actually do it are two completely different skills.

Certifications cover the second one thinly, and it's the one that decides almost every real outcome. This module is the half that gets skipped.

How people learn a movement

Beginners can absorb roughly one cue at a time. A list of five corrections produces worse performance than one, because attention is the bottleneck. Demonstrate, give one cue, let them try, then adjust. Feedback after every single rep also tends to slow learning in the long run — the learner stops self-correcting and waits to be told.

Internal versus external cues

An internal cue directs attention to the body; an external cue directs it to the effect of the movement on the environment. There's a reasonably consistent body of work suggesting external cues often produce better performance and retention, though the size of the advantage is debated and it may matter less for isolation work where the goal genuinely is to feel one muscle. effect size contested

Internal"Squeeze your glutes at the top"
External"Drive your hips toward the wall in front of you"
Internal"Push through your quads"
External"Push the floor away from you"
Internal"Retract your scapulae"
External"Pull your elbows into your back pockets"
Internal"Brace your core"
External"Act like you're about to be punched in the stomach"

Behaviour change

Self-efficacy

Belief that you can do the thing is one of the stronger predictors of whether you will. It's built by early, achievable wins — which is an argument for starting easier than feels necessary.

Identity over outcome

"I'm someone who trains on Tuesdays" outlasts "I want to lose 10 kg". Outcome goals end; identities don't.

Friction

Gym distance, bag packed, session written down in advance. Reducing small frictions reliably beats increasing motivation.

Lapse planning

Decide in advance what a missed week means. People who treat a lapse as data continue; people who treat it as failure quit.

The clinical technique behind much of this is motivational interviewing — drawing someone's own reasons for change out of them rather than supplying yours. Even a crude version of it outperforms being told what to do.

Training science 09

Pain, injury and who you can train

In plain words

This module is not about treating anything. It's about recognising what isn't yours to treat, and about not making things worse with careless language.

The single most useful idea here: tissue is adaptable, and pain is not a reliable readout of damage.

Load management

Tissue adapts to load applied gradually and fails when load outpaces adaptation. Most non-traumatic training complaints are a dose problem — too much, too soon, too fast a jump — rather than a technique flaw or a weak muscle. The first question for a niggle is almost always "what changed recently?"

Pain, briefly and carefully

Pain is produced by the nervous system, and correlates imperfectly with tissue damage. Imaging findings like disc bulges, cuff tears and cartilage changes appear in large numbers of entirely pain-free people, and their prevalence rises with age. well replicated This cuts both ways: it doesn't mean pain is imaginary, and it doesn't mean a scan finding is meaningless. It means a finding alone doesn't explain a symptom.

Practical consequence for anyone coaching: avoid frightening, mechanical language — "your spine is unstable", "your hips are out of alignment". It changes how people move and how much they do, generally for the worse.

Refer, don't diagnose. Pain that is severe, that wakes someone at night, that follows a specific traumatic incident, that comes with numbness, weakness, or loss of function, or that simply isn't improving — these go to a qualified clinician. No certification makes a trainer qualified to diagnose. This module doesn't either, and nothing here is medical advice.

Populations needing modification

Older adultsResistance training is strongly supported here, not contraindicated. Emphasis on strength, power and balance; slower progression, longer warm-ups
Pregnancy & postpartumGenerally encouraged with medical clearance; specific modifications by trimester and after delivery. Specialist territory — refer

Training science 10

Reading the evidence yourself

In plain words

Fitness has an unusually high ratio of confident claims to good evidence. This module is the skill that lets you sort them yourself, instead of trusting whoever sounds most certain.

It's the most durable thing in this whole app. Specific recommendations age; the ability to check them doesn't.

What makes a finding trustworthy

AskWhy
How many people, for how long?Training studies are often small and short. Twelve people over six weeks tells you less than the headline suggests
Who were they?Untrained beginners improve on almost anything. Findings in beginners rarely transfer to trained lifters
What was measured?Muscle activation (EMG) in one session is not muscle growth over months. Acute markers are not outcomes
How big was the effect?"Statistically significant" means unlikely to be chance, not meaningfully large. Ask for the size, not the p-value
Compared with what?Better than nothing is a low bar. Better than the normal alternative is the useful comparison
Who paid?Supplement and equipment funding doesn't invalidate a study, but it changes how much weight a single result deserves
Does it replicate?One study is a hypothesis. A systematic review of many is evidence

Training science 11

Pain during training — reading the signals

In plain words

Not everything that hurts is a problem, and not everything that's a problem hurts much. The useful skill isn't toughness or caution — it's telling the two apart.

This module is about recognising what you're feeling and knowing what to do next. It is not physiotherapy, and it deliberately contains no treatment protocols. Think of it as the triage step that happens before a professional gets involved.

Signals that mean stop and see someone — not wait and see. Pain following a distinct traumatic moment (a pop, a tear, a sudden giving way). Numbness, pins and needles, or shooting pain down a limb. Weakness you can't explain by fatigue. Swelling, a joint locking or giving way, or visible deformity. Pain that wakes you at night or is present at rest. Pain with fever, or with chest symptoms or breathlessness. None of these are things to train through or diagnose from an app.

How to handle the grey zone

Most training niggles aren't emergencies and aren't nothing. The modern approach is relative rest rather than complete rest: keep training, change the dose.

Change the range

Work in the range that doesn't provoke it. A box squat, a partial, a different depth — you keep the stimulus and skip the painful arc.

Change the load

Lighter with more reps often stays comfortable while keeping the pattern trained.

Substitute the exercise

Same pattern, different setup: trap bar instead of barbell, dumbbells instead of bar, machine instead of free weight.

Train everything else

A sore shoulder is not a reason to stop training legs. Total shutdown costs far more than it protects.

A commonly used self-monitoring approach in rehab settings is a traffic-light model: mild discomfort during a movement is acceptable, it shouldn't worsen as you go, and it should settle back to baseline by the following morning. If it's worse the next day, the dose was too high. This framework is widely used clinically, particularly in tendon rehabilitation clinical convention — I'd treat the specific thresholds as a rule of thumb rather than a validated rule, and anything persistent belongs with a clinician who can set it for your situation.

What's changed in recovery thinking

RICE — rest, ice, compression, elevation — is the acronym most people learned, and it has been substantially revised. The criticism is that prolonged rest and aggressive icing may interfere with the inflammatory process that healing depends on. A proposed replacement framework published in sports-medicine literature uses the acronyms PEACE and LOVE, emphasising protection and early gentle loading, avoiding anti-inflammatory suppression early on, education, and optimism, rather than extended immobilisation. I'm fairly confident this framework exists and is broadly as described, but I'd verify the detail from the original source before relying on it.

The durable takeaway, which is less contested than any acronym: early, gentle, graded loading generally beats extended rest for most soft-tissue training injuries. How early and how gentle is the clinician's call.

Where a trainer stops and a physiotherapist starts

A trainer manages load, technique, exercise selection and progression — all of which matter enormously for both preventing and working around problems. A physiotherapist assesses, diagnoses, and treats. The handover point is simple: the moment you're trying to work out what is wrong rather than how much to do, it's outside a trainer's scope.

Module 09 covers the broader pain-science background this module sits on. Neither is medical advice, and neither can assess your situation. If something is hurting and isn't resolving, see a physiotherapist or doctor — that's the correct answer, not a disclaimer.
